How are Chinese firms faring as AI and tech reshape global market cap rankings?

Tencent leads pack, but country's largest listed firms remain dominated by state-owned banks, energy producers and consumer brands

By Daisy Wu
Published: 7:00am, 23 Jun 2026

China's state-owned banks and energy majors – once shoulder to shoulder with American firms at the top of market capitalisation rankings – have fallen behind as AI and technology companies capture the imagination of global investors, redirecting capital and reshaping the global corporate order.

As of Friday, Nvidia topped the global rankings, with a market capitalisation of about US$5.1 trillion, followed by Alphabet and Apple. According to financial data provider CompaniesMarketCap, seven US technology firms were among the world's 10 largest listed companies, with Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co., Saudi Aramco and Samsung the only exceptions.

China's corporate landscape tells a different story. The largest listed firms remain dominated by state-owned banks, energy producers and consumer brands such as liquor giant Kweichow Moutai, though Tencent Holdings, the country's largest technology company, tops the domestic rankings at roughly US$505 billion.

Hong Hao, chief investment officer at Hong Kong-based Lotus Asset Management, said investors were increasingly willing to pay a premium for technology companies, while traditional industries were losing their appeal.

SpaceX's market debut this month offered the starkest illustration yet. The rocket company vaulted to the sixth-largest listed firm in the world, reinforcing investor conviction that artificial intelligence and technology are redrawing the boundaries of global capital.

"The old economy is fading and everyone wants tech," Hong said. "These are the signs of the time."

The shift marks a reversal from two decades ago. PetroChina became the world's most valuable listed company shortly after its 2007 Shanghai debut, with a market value exceeding US$1 trillion. Today, it no longer ranks among the world's top 50 by market capitalisation.

Industrial and Commercial Bank of China and China Construction Bank once featured near the top of the global rankings but now sit around the middle of the global top 100, with market capitalisations of roughly US$311 billion and US$383 billion, respectively.

A report released by accounting firm PwC in April said the combined market value of the world's 100 largest listed companies reached a record US$51.8 trillion in March, up 22 per cent year on year, driven largely by technology-related gains.

The silver lining for China is that more private sector giants have ridden the country's promotion of manufacturing and technology to maintain global scale. Contemporary Amperex Technology Ltd, the world's largest electric-vehicle battery maker, has a market capitalisation of about US$276 billion, with e-commerce giant Alibaba Group Holding, the owner of the South China Morning Post, close behind.

"AI-related sectors are capturing a lot of hype and hopes," Hong said. "A bigger market cap does not mean better fundamentals. It's different earnings and multiples."

A new generation of Chinese AI companies is beginning to emerge. Start-ups such as Zhipu – whose market cap topped HK$1 trillion (US$127.6 billion) on Monday – are drawing investor attention, though their scale remains far below that of their leading US counterparts.

"China's AI push and investment in advanced manufacturing could gradually shift its market composition from financials-heavy to innovation-heavy," said Jacky Tang, chief investment officer for emerging markets at Deutsche Bank's Hong Kong branch.

"But this will likely be an incremental rebalancing rather than a regime shift, as financials and state-owned enterprises will remain the core of index composition."

He added that the valuation gap between US and Chinese equities remained wide, with the collective value of the top 10 US companies roughly seven to eight times as much as that of their Chinese counterparts.

Analysts caution, however, that soaring market capitalisations do not necessarily translate into strong long-term returns, with concerns that enthusiasm surrounding AI may be running ahead of fundamentals.

David Chao, global market strategist for Asia-Pacific at Invesco, struck a similar note, warning that while AI fundamentals remained broadly sound, markets were priced for perfection – leaving little room for error.

"If we do hit a speed bump – whether that be a semiconductor company not upgrading its AI guidance or a major bottleneck in energy production or potential regulatory changes that could impact the growth outlook – the correction could be painful," he said.
